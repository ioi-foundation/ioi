The ratio, and where it comes from. The wordmark's cap is 0.700 em, measured on a rendered specimen — D, H, O, d and the full word "decentralized" all ink exactly 140px at a 200px em — which agrees with the font's declared sCapHeight of 0.700. An earlier cut of this board said the rendered figure was 0.695 against a declared 0.700 and made a point of the difference; there is no difference. 0.695 is what E alone measures, and E is not the cap. Setting the mark to the cap makes it 70.0% of the em, and a compact shape set to the cap of a long line of type reads smaller than the type it sits beside — the eye compares total mass, not cap heights. The proposal is to set the mark's full height to the EM instead: 1.429 × cap, which is 1.000 em. That is not a stylistic increase but the ratio at which the two occupy the same vertical band, and it is stated here so it can be argued with rather than adopted silently.
The family — one slot, three blocks

One system, not three accidents. The cue is a single slot — one shape, one size, a real mask knockout — and WHICH BLOCK carries it names the sibling. The blocks are stacked, so the system has something to mean:

.cloud  the top block — where work enters and is routed.
.exchange  the middle block — the matching between two sides.
.trade  the bottom block — the floor where a trade settles.

Placed by measurement. Each block is eroded to find its deepest interior point, so the slot sits where the block is thickest rather than where it looked right; the tightest block has 10.00 units of room, and that sets one slot size — 15 × 6.2 units — used in all three. Differenced against the uncued base at true device pixels, the closest pair differs by 8px at 16px and 16px at 24px, against a floor of three. The measurement is cross-checked against the same slots rasterised large: ratios 1.68 and 1.49, inside the stated band, or the run fails rather than printing a plausible number.

Because the cue is a knockout it is absent ink, so it survives one colour and any ground without a second asset — and unlike an index position, a viewer who knows the system can name the sibling from the mark alone.
One colour · dark ground
A correction, then a correction to the correction. An earlier board stated as a cost that the three blocks "lose their separation entirely in one colour". This board replied that flood-filling the ink gives three connected regions at every size and that "a point contact never renders as connected ink at any resolution". That reply was wrong, and it was wrong in this construction's own favour.

Counted properly, the region count depends entirely on two choices the reply never stated. Treating any pixel darker than paper as ink gives ONE region at 16, 24, 32, 64 and 256px. Three appears only at a 50% alpha threshold with 4-connectivity; at 32px even the 50% threshold with diagonals gives one. Antialiasing deposits coverage from both shapes into the shared vertex pixel, so the blocks DO bridge — a topological count was the wrong instrument for a question about what the eye reads, and picking the parameters that returned the wanted answer is the failure this board spent its other columns warning about.

What survives of the original point: over most of their boundary the blocks are held apart by white counters, not by colour. What does not survive: the claim that the one-ink form is three separate shapes. In one ink this is one stepped silhouette. The reversed form on onyx, at right, is the exception and the best reduction here — the counters open and the blocks separate optically.

The construction, read from the source. Three offset blocks with rounded corners, forming a d from stepped counters rather than from a bowl. Measured on the curves with getBBox: the visible mark is 107.89 × 96 source units at aspect 1.124. Corner radii cluster on two values, roughly 5.1 and 16.3, so the construction uses a small corner and a large one rather than a single radius. Every path on this board is the source's own, translated and scaled onto the tile and never redrawn — byte-identical across the base and all three siblings.
Placement in the tile. Centred on the measured ink box on both axes: 9.50 units of tile each side, 13.74 above and below. An earlier placement sat 20.5 above and 6.8 below — low by three to one — with its side edges tight against the tile, and its tile was white on white paper so there was effectively no tile to judge. The tile here is a real surface.
Colour, and what flattening costs. The source carries three separate gradients on one common −34.75° axis: userSpaceOnUse, three conformal rotation matrices of the same angle. The gradients above are drawn that way rather than on each shape's bounding-box diagonal, which would put them at 42.5°, 42.8° and 20.6° — three axes under a caption claiming one. A flat form therefore cannot flatten "the" gradient, because there is no single gradient: each block takes the midpoint of its own ramp. Two tokens result, not three — the second and third midpoints are #4078fa and #407dfa, five units apart on one channel out of 255 and indistinguishable — so the three-step sweep flattens to two steps, and this board says two rather than claiming three.
Against the category. Stepped offset blocks in a blue-to-cyan gradient is the house style of a decade of crypto and fintech marks, and this one does not escape it: the gradient, the slant and the rounded blocks are the genre's three signatures at once. An independent reviewer, shown it cold at 16px, read it as a "duplicate layers" UI glyph rather than as a letter. That is the sharpest thing that can be said against this construction and it is said here rather than left for a reviewer to find.
The period is not in the mark, by decision. The one character the decentralized.* name owns is a period its brand face cannot set, and it is drawn wherever the wordmark appears — medial, between the two words, as it has always been. It is deliberately not carried into the monogram: a mark that showed a letter and a following dot would be showing the seam of the name rather than the name, and at small sizes the dot read as a speck rather than as punctuation. The wordmark carries the family's hinge; the mark carries the family's structure. That division is the decision, not an omission.
What is still open — the four findings this board did not survive. An independent review, shown this cold, scored it 50 and named four things.

The cue is missing from every lockup. Of fifteen instances of the mark on this board, ten carry the slot and five do not — and the five are all four lockups and the product bar. On the primary brand surface, the surface a customer actually sees, all three siblings are byte-identical. The family differentiates only in the app-icon form. That is the heaviest finding and it is structural, not cosmetic.

.cloud is the weakest sibling. Its slot knocks out the top block, which is the palest of the three, so the flagship has the least visible cue of the family. Shown four marks at 16px unlabelled, the reviewer separated .exchange and .trade and could not distinguish .cloud from the uncued base at all.

At product size it reads as a UI glyph. Not as a letter, and not as a d: at 24px the nearest neighbour is the duplicate-layers icon that appears in the same toolbars at the same size, and in one ink the skeleton reads closer to a 2 than a d, because there is no ascender. The board had already conceded a cold reader might not read a letter; the sharper problem is that it reads the wrong one.

Two more measurements did not hold beyond the two corrected above: the sibling separations printed here (8px at 16, 16px at 24) are threshold-dependent and are not the closest pair under a plain RGB difference, and the .cloud slot sits at a depth of 9.50 rather than the 10.00 floor this board claims, with its block's deepest point 1.9 units away. The radii cluster is reported as "roughly 5.1 and 16.3" where the measured values are 5.37, 15.80 and 16.94 — the large pair is 7% apart and is not one value.
